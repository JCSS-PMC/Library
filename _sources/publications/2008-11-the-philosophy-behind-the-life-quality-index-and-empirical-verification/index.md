## 1. Preface

<p>The following memorandum collects and discusses most of the relevant material presently available for setting up rational risk acceptance criteria and risk reduction expenses. They are based on the so-called life quality index and utility considerations. The paper attempts to support all basic assumptions by empirical findings. Much of the material presented herein is not available elsewhere. The memorandum is meant to be the basis for practical decisions.</p>

<p>Before starting we try to estimate the size of risk reductions we are going to discuss. Overall crude mortality (per year) is about 0.01 in industrial countries but only 3 in 10000 are not due to natural causes. If one subtracts from this number those deaths which are induced by voluntary risky activities (sports and some traffic accidents) and those which are unavoidable such as house accidents, climbing stairs, etc., then, the reduction of a mortality of about 0.0002 or less is the subject of our study. It may be a little larger because certain risks typical for industrialized countries like air pollution are not separated out in the usual statistical records.</p>

<p>Also, we estimate the result of a (crude) mortality reduction <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0001">\(dm/\mathit{year}\)</span> in terms of increases in life expectancy <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0002">\(d\ell\)</span> using a European life table (the corresponding theory is developed later).</p>

## 2. Derivations and Empirical Verification of the Life Quality Index

<p>The following considerations are valid only for public reduction of involuntary risks of an anonymous member of society. If we wish to recommend rational choices monetary valuation of human life cannot be avoided. Attributes like life expectancy, age, income and consumption must be incorporated. In particular, let <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0003">\(g&gt;0\)</span> be the fraction of the real (inflation-free) GDP available for risk reduction and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0004">\(\ell&gt;0\)</span> the life expectancy at birth. A factor adjusting <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0005">\(\ell\)</span> for life years in good health can be included if necessary [40] . In order to clarify the essentials of the life quality index all steps of derivation are presented. Long life and consumption (wealth) are, in fact, among the primary concerns of humans in a modern society as shown by Cantril [15] and many similar, more recent empirical studies.</p>

<p>Assume that the life quality index <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0006">\(L,\)</span> a suitable composite social indicator, is the product of a function of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0007">\(g\)</span> (as a measure of the quality of life) and another function of the time <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0008">\(t=(1-w)\ell\)</span> to enjoy life (as a measure of the quantity of life) where <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0009">\(w\ (0&lt;w&lt;1)\)</span> is the time (as a fraction of life expectancy) to be spend in (paid) work. Thus, the new variable <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0010">\(w\)</span> is also considered as an indicator of life quality. The individual can now increase leisure time by either increasing life expectancy by risk reduction or by reducing the time spent in economic production which generally means smaller income. Assume then that the quantity <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0011">\(w\)</span> is chosen such that <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0012">\(L\)</span> is maximized. This appears to be a reasonable assumption because most work is dull, boring, troublesome and sometimes dangerous. One also can draw on a historical argument. In 1870 the yearly time spent in work was 2900 hours, in 1950 still 2000 but at present only 1600 on average. Simultaneously, life expectancy rose from 45 to almost 80 years due to the advances in medical sciences, nutrition and sanitary installations (which in this context must be considered as exogenous) and the GDP per capita increased from some 2000 PPPUS$ well beyond 20000 PPPUS$ due to higher productivity [46] <sup><a id="lqi-note-ref-1" href="#lqi-note-1" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>. Higher life quality, therefore, was not only achieved through longer lives and higher consumption but also by significantly more leisure time. In fact, it has also been argued repeatedly on philosophical, sociological and economical grounds that time for leisure (under good economical conditions as well as in good health) is the ultimate source of life quality [95] .</p>

<p>The real (i.e. net of inflation) gross domestic product (GDP) per capita and year is a common indicator of the economic status and behavior of a country. The GDP can be considered as a surrogate measure of wealth-related aspects of quality of life (as synonymous with standard of living). It is a suitable indicator of the economic aspects of quality of life. The GDP is roughly the sum of all incomes created by labor and capital (stored labor) in a country during a year. In most OECD countries about three quarters of the GDP are raised by labor and the rest by capital. Any transfer cost are carefully eliminated. Other equivalent definitions are: GDP = all expenditures on goods and services by the groups in society (households, businesses, all levels of government, foreigners), or GDP = production of goods by various industries - agriculture, manufacturing, wholesale, retail trade, etc.[7] . The GDP provides the infrastructure of a country, its social structure, its cultural and educational offers, the sustainability of the economy, its ecological conditions among others but also the means for the individual enjoyment of life by consumption in its various forms. In most developed countries about <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0013">\(60\pm10\%\)</span> of the GDP is used privately, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0014">\(15\pm5\%\)</span> by the state (e.g. for military, police, jurisdiction, education, etc.) without transfer payments and the rest for (re- )investments. In most cases net exports can be neglegted. The GDP also creates the possibilities to ’’purchase’’ additional life years through better medical care, improved safety in road and railway traffic, more safety in or around building facilities, more safety from hazardous technical activities, more safety from natural hazards, etc.. It does not matter whether those investments into ’’life saving’’ are carried out individually, voluntarily or enforced by regulation or by the state via taxes. Neither a minimum share for the state nor the investments into depreciating production means can be reduced appreciably. State consumption forms a large part of the (external) conditions for the individual to enjoy life in high quality, now and in the future. Continuing investments is a necessary condition for a sustainable economy. Therefore, only the part for private use is available for risk reduction as a minimum. The part available for risk reduction then is a little less than <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0016 lqi-inline-0017">\(g\approx0.6 GDP\)</span> in first approximation and as a lower bound. A probably rather realistic estimate for <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0018">\(g\)</span> in terms of the GDP is obtained by leaving the investments untouched but take the government share without any transfer and subsidizing (social) payments giving <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0019">\(g\approx0.7\,GDP\)</span> or a little larger (see fig. 1 for an example [85] ). An upper bound clearly is the GDP itsself. The exact share for risk reduction must be determined separately for each country or group in a country and requires great care. The public must decide how much it is willing to spend on risk reduction, how much it is willing to give up of other public services and how much it wants to use for private consumption.</p>

<p>Some elegant mathematical derivation in Nathwani et al. [44] leads to the traditional form of the <strong>Life</strong> <strong>Quality</strong> <strong>Index</strong> <strong>(LQI)</strong></p>

(lqi-display-001)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[L_w=g^w\ell^{1-w}(1-w)^{1-w}\tag{1}\]
```

<p>The fraction of time <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0020">\(w\)</span> of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0021">\(\ell\)</span> necessary for paid work varies between less than 0.1 and 0.25 (see [63] for estimates of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0022">\(w\)</span> for different countries but also [51] and [46] ). Eq. (1) is rederived in what follows. Nathwani et al. [44] start from a simple product <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0023">\(L=f(g)h(t)\)</span> with <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0024">\(t=(1-w)\ell\)</span> the fraction of life devoted to leisure (or other non-productive time, e.g. sleep) and, consequently, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0025">\(w\ell\)</span> the fraction of life devoted to paid work. Thus, the LQI is a product of a function <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0026">\(f(g)\)</span> measuring life quality and a function <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0027">\(h(t)\)</span> measuring the duration of enjoyment of life. <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0028">\(f(g)\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0029">\(h(t)\)</span> are assumed to be independent, differential and monotonically increasing functions. Income and life expectancy are highly correlated across countries (see fig. 2). However, they are only weakly (and positively) correlated in countries with a developed social welfare system (see table 1 where it should be noted that the incomes in the highest quantile differ from those in the lowest quantile by a factor of 7 to 10 that is roughly the same range of incomes as the GDP’s in fig.2). The LQI as a product of two independent factors, therefore, is justified in good approximation.</p>

<p>Introduce the differential <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0030">\(dL=\frac{\partial f(g)}{\partial g}h(t)\,dg+\frac{\partial h(t)}{\partial t}f(g)\,dt\)</span> supposing that interest is in small changes in the LQI. Defining relative changes in the LQI by <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0031 lqi-inline-0032 lqi-inline-0033 lqi-inline-0034 lqi-inline-0035">\(\frac{dL}{L}=\frac{g}{f(g)} \frac{df(g)}{dg}\frac{dg}{g}+\frac{t}{h(t)} \frac{dh(t)}{dt} \frac{dt}{t}=k_g\frac{dg}{g}+ k_t\frac{dt}{t}\)</span> and setting <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0036">\(k_g/k_t=\mathit{const.}\)</span> according to the universality requirement in [44] (indifference of the relative impact of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0037">\(g\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0038">\(t\)</span> on life quality with respect to the actual values of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0039">\(g\)</span> and t), one finds two differential equations <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0040 lqi-inline-0041 lqi-inline-0042">\(k_g\equiv\frac{g}{f(g)} \frac{df(g)}{dg} =r\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0043 lqi-inline-0044 lqi-inline-0045">\(k_t\equiv\frac{t}{h(t)} \frac{dh(t)}{dt} =s\)</span> with solutions <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0046">\(f(g)=g^r\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0047">\(h(t)=t^s=((1-w)\ell)^s\ (r,s&gt;0).\)</span> This gives <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0048">\(L=g^r((1-w)\ell)^s\)</span> apart from proportionality constants. Nathwani et al. [44] then assumed <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0049">\(g\propto pw\)</span> where <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0050">\(p\)</span> is proportional to labor productivity (monetary output per hour worked) and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0051">\(g\)</span> is wealth production per year. Note the linearity of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0052">\(g\)</span> on <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0053">\(w.\)</span> Other factors also affecting wealth production like capital, land and resources are merged into a proportionality constant.</p>

<p><em>’’Presumably,</em> <em>people</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0054"><em>on</em></span> <em>the</em> <em>average</em> <em>work</em> <em>just</em> <em>enough</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0055"><em>so</em></span> <em>that</em> <em>the</em> <em>marginal</em> <em>value</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0056"><em>of</em></span> <em>wealth</em> <em>pro</em><em>duced,</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0057"><em>or</em></span> <em>income</em> <em>earned,</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0058"><em>is</em></span> <em>equal</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0059"><em>to</em></span> <em>the</em> <em>marginal</em> <em>value</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0060"><em>of</em></span> <em>the</em> <em>time</em> <em>they</em> <em>lose</em> <em>when</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0061"><em>at</em></span> <em>work</em> ’’ as hypothesized by Nathwani et al.[44] . Standard economic models of labor supply also assume that the value of leisure time at the margin is equal to the marginal wage rate [7] . More directly, Lind [40] stated: <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0062"><em>A</em></span> <em>prospect</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0063"><em>to</em></span> <em>save</em> <em>life</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0064"><em>or</em></span> <em>produce</em> <em>wealth</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0065"><em>is</em></span> <em>preferable</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0066"><em>in</em></span> <em>comparison</em> <em>with</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0067"><em>an</em></span> <em>alternative</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0068"><em>if</em></span> <em>the</em> <em>net</em> <em>increase</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0069"><em>in</em></span> <em>life</em> <em>expectancy</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0070"><em>is</em></span> <em>greater</em> <em>than</em> <em>its</em> <em>work</em> <em>time</em> <em>cost.</em> Consequently, people who produce wealth by work, possibly together with their families, optimize work and non-productive time, i.e. their LQI. From <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0071 lqi-inline-0072">\(\frac{dL}{dw}=\frac{d}{dw}(pw)^r[(1-w)\ell]^s=\frac{r}{w}-\frac{s}{1-w} =0\)</span> for <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0073">\(\ell=\mathit{const.}&gt;0\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0074">\(p=\mathit{const.}&gt;0\)</span> one determines <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0075">\(r=s\frac{w}{1-w}\)</span> which, without loss of generality, together with <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0076">\(r+s=1\)</span> results in <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0077">\(r=w\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0078">\(s=1-w\)</span> and</p>

(lqi-display-002)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[L_w=(pw)^r\ell^{1-r}(1-w)^{1-r}\approx g^r\ell^{1-r}\tag{2}\]
```

<p>with <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0079">\(g=pw.\)</span> Also, it is <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0080 lqi-inline-0081">\(\left.\frac{d^2L}{dw^2}\right|_{r=1-s=w} &lt;0\)</span> for <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0082">\(r,s&gt;0.\ w\ (w&gt;0)\)</span> can be assumed to be almost constant at a given time so that the factor <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0083">\((1-w)^{1-r}\)</span> can be dropped in many applications. Assuming that <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0084">\(w\)</span> is already at its optimum value <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0085">\(w^*\)</span> according to the work-leisure optimization principle just stated before we choose <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0086">\(r=w^*.\)</span> For later convenience we take the <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0087">\((1-w^*\)</span>)-th root in the numerator of eq. (1) and introduce the notation <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0088">\(q^*=\frac{w^*}{1-w^*}.\)</span> Additionally, we divide <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0089">\(g^{q^*}\)</span> by <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0090">\(q^*.\)</span> Dividing <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0091">\(g^{q^*}\)</span> by <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0092">\(q^*\)</span> removes a minor inconsistency of the original form for all practical purposes because persons with the same <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0093">\(g\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0094">\(\ell\)</span> but larger <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0095">\(w^*\)</span> would have higher life quality (see also section 3). Apart from multiplicative constants we then have:</p>

(lqi-display-003)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[L_q=\frac{g^{q^*}}{q^*}\ell(1-w^*)\tag{3}\]
```

<p><span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0096">\(L_q\)</span> fulfills the boundary conditions <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0097">\(L_q=0\)</span> for <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0098">\(g=0\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0099">\(\ell=0.\ L_q\)</span> can and should be interpreted as a lifetime utility function <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0100">\(L_q=u(g,\ell,w)\)</span> of an anonymous person. Viewed as a utility function the form eq. (3) has the desirable property of being risk-neutral with respect to life expectancy in contrast to eq. (2).</p>

<p>Nathwani et al.’s simple and straightforward reasoning has been scrutinized repeatedly by several investigators. One such investigation deserves special attention as it ends up with a slightly different form of the life quality index. Ditlevsen/Friis-Hansen [21] started from a general differential equation for the relative increments for the allocation of time for paid work <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0101">\(pw\ell\)</span> and for leisure time</p>

(lqi-math-06)=
<div class="native-source-content" id="lqi-math-06" data-source-math="lqi-math-06">

<p><span class="math notranslate nohighlight">\((1-w)\ell\)</span>, i.e. from <span class="math notranslate nohighlight">\(\frac{dA}{A}=f\left(\frac{d(pw\ell)}{pw\ell},\frac{d((1-w)\ell)}{(1-w)\ell}\right)\)</span> which must have the form <span class="math notranslate nohighlight">\(\frac{dA}{A}=c_1\frac{d(pw\ell)}{pw\ell}+c_2\frac{d((1-w)\ell)}{(1-w)\ell}\)</span> (where after division by <span class="math notranslate nohighlight">\(c_1+c_2\)</span> it follows that new constants are <span class="math notranslate nohighlight">\(r=\frac{c_1}{c_1+c_2}\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight">\(s=\frac{c_2}{c_1+c_2}=1-r\)</span>)</p>

</div>

<p>with solution</p>

(lqi-display-004)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[L_{w\ell}=(pw)^r(1-w)^{1-r}\ell\tag{4}\]
```

<p>as can easily be verified. <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0102">\(r\)</span> is some unknown combination coefficient. If this is determined by the work-leisure optimization principle, i.e. from <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0103">\(\frac{dL_{w\ell}}{dw}=\frac{d}{dw}\bigl((pw)^r(1-w)^{1-r}\ell\bigr)=0,\)</span> one obtains <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0104">\(r=w^*\)</span> with <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0105">\(g=pw^*\)</span> as before. Similar to eq. (3) it is convenient to divide this equation by the exponent of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0106">\(g,w^*,\)</span> so that finally</p>

(lqi-display-005)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[L_{w\ell}=\frac{1}{w^*}g^{w^*}(1-w^*)^{1-w^*}\ell\tag{5}\]
```

<p>apart from proportionality constants. Note that for given <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0107">\(p\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0108">\(\ell\)</span> eq. (4) or (2) are functions of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0109">\(w\)</span> while eq. (5) or (3) are numbers in which <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0110">\(w=w^*\)</span> is the actual (observed) value of the work fraction. Ditlevsen/Friis-Hansen denoted this new index by <em>Life</em> <em>quality</em> <em>time</em> <em>allocation</em> <em>index</em> (LQTAI). Risk-neutrality with respect to life expectancy comes out directly. Their derivation does not need to impose the constraint <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0111">\(k_g/k_t=\mathit{const.}\)</span> but the constraint is implicitely present because <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0112">\(r\)</span> in eq. (4) must be constant. But they use the whole life for an allocation of work time and leisure time. If this is chosen in the derivations by Nathwani et al., i.e. if the work-leisure optimization principle is applied to the deterministic life time utility <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0113">\(L=(pw\ell)^r\ell^{1-r}(1-w)^{1-r}.\)</span> eq. (1) and (5) coincide. However, the derivation of the LQTAI does not directly allow an interpretation as a utility function. It is here where the concepts used by Nathwani et al. and Ditlevsen/Friis-Hansen differ. Otherwise, the two derivations are completely equivalent. Unfortunately, it will be almost impossible to verify one or the other form empirically. The question which version to select will be taken up again in section 6 <sup><a id="lqi-note-ref-2" href="#lqi-note-2" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>.</p>

<p>The derivations can easily be extended to a non-linear relationship between <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0114">\(g\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0115">\(w\)</span> as pointed out recently by [56] . A non-linear relationship has been proposed in [20] but such relationships have a long history in economics [47] . The widely-used two-factor Cobb-Douglas production function represents any output, the output of a firm or the GDP in a macroeconomic context, as <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0116">\(Q=AK^\alpha L^\beta\)</span> where <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0117">\(A\)</span> is a so-called technology constant, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0118">\(L\)</span> is labor input, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0119">\(K\)</span> is capital input and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0120">\(0&lt;\alpha,\beta\leq1\)</span> in order to have dimishishing returns in each variable accounting for the fact, that implementation of more labor or more capital becomes increasingly inefficient for given technology. At a given time <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0121">\(A\)</span> is exogenous and constant. <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0122">\(A\)</span> is not or only indirectly a controllable variable for decision-making. It depends on technological progress and how a society pushes it forward by clever use of labor and capital. Usually, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0123">\(\alpha+\beta=1\)</span> is taken meaning constant returns to scale (doubling both inputs doubles output). <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0124">\(\alpha+\beta\gtrless\)</span> implies increasing/decreasing returns to scale. The marginal product of capital is <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0125">\(MPK=\frac{\partial Q}{\partial K}=\alpha AK^{\alpha-1}L^{1-\alpha}=\alpha\frac{Q}{K}\)</span> and, similarly, the marginal product of labor is <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0126">\(MPL=\frac{\partial Q}{\partial L}=(1-\alpha)AK^\alpha L^{-\alpha}=(1-\alpha)\frac{Q}{L}.\)</span> Therefore, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0127">\(\beta=1-\alpha\)</span> can be computed as the ratio of labor output to total output because the labor share of total output is <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0128">\(MPL*L.\ \beta\)</span> turns out to be almost constant over the past decades with a slight trend to smaller <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0129">\(\beta&#x27;\)</span>s in recent years. This constancy is evidence for the validity of the Cobb-Douglas function. For example, for the US, Canada, Switzerland and Germany <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0130">\(\beta\)</span> is very close to <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0131">\(0.7\)</span> over the last 20 to 40 years (see fig. 3). In our notation, it is then <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0132">\(g\sim cw^\beta\)</span> with <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0133">\(c=A(k)^\alpha\ (k=\frac{K}{N},N\)</span> =population size). Performing the same manipulations as before and collecting all constants in a new constant one finds for eq. (2)</p>

(lqi-display-006)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[r=\frac{w^*}{\beta-\beta w^*+w^*}\tag{6}\]
```

<p>and, because <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0134">\(q=\frac{r}{1-r}\)</span> with <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0135">\(r\)</span> in eq. (6),</p>

(lqi-display-007)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[q^*=\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{w^*}{1-w^*}\tag{7}\]
```

<p>in eq. (3). Although the validity of the Cobb-Douglas production function is discussed repeatedly in the literature there is good empirical evidence that it is at least a good approximation in many applications and for many countries.</p>

<p>The work-leisure optimization principle adopted before is central for the determination of the exponents in the two terms of the life quality index. Apart from the fact that the optimization principle makes sense intuitively, at least in the long run, and is clearly supported by the abovementioned historical development of wealth, life expectancy and working time the crucial question is whether one can find other empirical evidence that the work-leisure optimization principle is indeed working. If it is active the second question is whether societies are already at the optimum. Some indirectly supporting material is given in [10] for European countries. For example, Bielenski et al. [10] show by a representative inquiry of up to 3000 people in each of the different countries that people tend to prefer less work time in countries with a high GDP but would prefer more work and thus more income in countries with lower GDP (possibly with larger unemployment rate and less work volume offered by the economy). Dependent full-time employees in countries with high GDP prefer to reduce their weekly working time from some 38.5 hours down to 34 to 36 hours. Self-employed people would like to reduce their work load from 48 hours down to 38 hours. The dependence between income and preferred weekly working time can be seen from the analysis in [10] on a household level (see table 2). In countries with a high GDP there is a clear tendency to redistribute the available work volume among a larger labor force (especially females with part-time work) in order to reduce the individual work load. In table 3 current and preferred working time is tabulated for some countries. If preferred working time is smaller than the actual one the income level is supposed to remain constant, if it is larger more income is desired. These tendencies are also supported by recent official labor statistics [51] . In [22] it is stated that in some countries full-time employees voted for weekly working times not below 35 hours.</p>

## 3. LQI as a Substitute for the HDI

<p>The LQI initially was developed to be used as an alternative for the human development index (HDI) [?] as a measure of ’’how well a nation serves the well-being of its citizens’’. Then, it must be calculated with the full GDP, of course. Fig. 6 displays various versions of the index over the (optimum) <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0153">\(w.\)</span> It is seen that the original LQI in eq. (1) divided by <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0154">\(w\)</span> fulfills the reasonable requirement that more work decreases life quality, at least within the range of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0155">\(w\)</span> to be expected in practice. It is also in this context that Nathwani et al.’s ethical requirement of indifference of the relative impact of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0156">\(g\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0157">\(t\)</span> on life quality with respect to the actual values of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0158">\(g\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0159">\(t\)</span> makes good sense. But the versions eq. (3) or eq. (3) with (7) are not suitable while the original version with Cobb-Douglas correction before taking the <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0160">\(\frac{w}{\beta-\beta w+w}\)</span> −th root is again acceptable, i.e.</p>

(lqi-display-008)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[L_{w,\beta}=\frac{g^{\frac{w}{\beta-\beta w+w}}}{\frac{w}{\beta-\beta w+w}}\ell^{1-\frac{w}{\beta-\beta w+w}}(1-w)^{1-\frac{w}{\beta-\beta w+w}}\tag{8}\]
```

<p>Also, if used in this context the LQI should be appropriately normalized (see also [41] ). Unfortunately, there is no obvious procedure to normalize it.</p>

## 4. Acceptability criteria

<p>Shepard/Zeckhauser and others [75] [39] derived the <em>willingness-to-pay</em> from the condition that a change in life expectancy and the corresponding change in consumption balance each other by keeping the life quality index (or the remaining life time utility) constant, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0161">\(L=L(\ell,g)=\mathit{const.}\)</span> or, equivalently,</p>

(lqi-display-009)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[dL=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\ell}\,d\ell+\frac{\partial L}{\partial g}\,dg=0\tag{9}\]
```

<p>so that after rearrangement</p>

(lqi-display-010)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[WTP=-dg=-\frac{\frac{\partial L}{\partial\ell}}{\frac{\partial L}{\partial g}}\,d\ell\]
```

<p>Inserting eq. (3) leads to</p>

(lqi-display-011)=

```{math}
:nowrap:

\[dg=-\frac{g}{q}\frac{d\ell}{\ell}\quad\text{or}\quad\frac{dg}{g}+\frac{1}{q}\frac{d\ell}{\ell}\geq0\tag{10}\]
```

<p>Criteria of the type (10) remain unaffected by multiplicative constants such as the productivity <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0162">\(p\)</span> or multiplicative corrections of life expectancy as proposed in health related economic studies in order to adjust life expectancy for lifetimes in bad health (which very well enter numerically into the LQI). Also, those criteria are independent of monotone transformations as done, for example, in eq. (3). Eq. (9) sometimes is also called the ’’LQI invariance principle’’. The equality in (10) gives an indication of what is <strong>necessary</strong> and <strong>affordable</strong> to a society for life saving undertakings; projects having ’’&lt;’’ are not admissible. The latter projects would, in fact, be life-consuming and,</p>

Whenever a given incremental increase in life expectancy by some life saving operation (positive <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0164">\(d\ell)\)</span> is associated with larger than optimal incremental cost (negative <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0165">\(dg)\)</span> one should invest into alternatives of life saving. ## 5. Sustainable Discounting

<p>In view of the time horizon of some 20 to more than 100 years (i.e. several generations) for civil engineering facilities discount rates should be long-term averages. They should be net of inflation and taxes. Investments into risk reduction must be discounted as other investments in order to avoid inconsistencies [58] . In the private sector a long term real interest rate is roughly identical to the (maximum) return rate one could get from an investment. But can the public also adopt such a strategy? The public does not make financial profit except by its economical growth. Public interest rates vary greatly among countries and appear to be set according to quite different rationales. For information, table 5 gives some discount rates used by government agencies in different countries. These rates are meant for comparing alternatives. This table clearly shows that there is hardly a common rationale behind setting public interest rates.</p>

<p>Recently, much discussion has been taken place in the context of sustainable development, long term public investments in general and intergenerational justice - aspects which appear very relevant in our context. Weinstein/Stason [92] and others require that interest rates for life saving interventions should be the same as for other cost and thus equal to the real market interest rate, simply for consistency reasons. This appears to be an extreme point of view. The other extreme of not discounting intergenerationally at all is expressed, for example, in [14] and [72] , based primarily on ethical grounds in the context of CO<span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0171"><sub>2</sub>-</span> induced global warming, nuclear waste disposals, depletion of natural resources, etc.. But greenhouse gas abatement and other long term public investments are probably somewhat different from our goal of risk reduction as pointed out in [73] . But discounting must be considered as a fact of life and modern economic mechanisms [60] .</p>

<p>The real public interest rate is strongly related to the benefit a society earns from the various activities of its members, i.e. its real economic growth per capita (see also [63] where the interest rate has been set equal to the growth rate). It is important to take it per capita because it then reflects the counteracting effects of economic and population growth. In the economics literature this is sometimes called the ’’natural interest rate’’. The United Nations Human Development Report 2001 [?] gives values between 1.2 and 1.9 % for industrialized countries during 1975-1998. If one considers the last 120 years and the data in [46] for some selected countries one determines a growth rate per capita of about 1.8% for Western Europe and the so-called Western Offshoots, USA, Canada and Australia, and a little more for Japan. The long time period of 120 years is justified as it covers the period of industrialization including some economic crisis and several times of war and postwar years. The same period is also approximately the time for which risk reducing interventions should be valid.</p>

<p>Modern economic growth theory has been widely applied to sustainability financing. Nordhaus [45] and others (see [81] for an overview but also the other papers in Energy Policy, 23, 3/4, 1995 as well as Int. J. Sustainable Development, 6,1, 2003) follow the classical Ramseyan approach (see [68] [77] and [8] ) for optimal stable economic growth in perfect markets</p>

(lqi-display-012)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[\gamma=\rho+\epsilon\delta>0\tag{11}\]
```

<p>where <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0172">\(\gamma\)</span> is the real market interest rate, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0173">\(\rho&gt;0\)</span> the rate of pure time preference of consumption, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0174">\(\epsilon\)</span> the elasticity of marginal consumption (income) and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0175">\(\delta\)</span> the consumption (income) growth rate per capita. Clearly, the subjective element is the quantity <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0176">\(\rho.\)</span> The rate <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0177">\(\rho\)</span> has been interpreted as the effect of human impatience, myopia, egoism, lack of telescopic faculty, etc.. Its existence in human behavior has been widely demonstrated in human ethology and economics [60] . It is partially justified because there is uncertainty about one’s future. However, there are many authors in economics as well as philosophical and political sciences including Ramsey who refuse convincingly to accept a rate <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0178">\(\rho&gt;0\)</span> in intergenerational contexts on ethical grounds (see, for example, [14] [72] [61] ) while it is considered fully acceptable for intragenerational discounting. On the other hand, intergenerational equity arguments in Arrow [2] indicate that there should be a <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0179">\(\rho&gt;0\)</span> in order to remove an ’’... <em>incredible</em> <em>and</em> <em>unacceptable</em> <em>strain</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0180"><em>on</em></span> <em>the</em> <em>present</em> <em>generation</em> ’’ Rabl [61] argues that there must be 0 <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0181">\(&lt;\gamma&lt;\epsilon\delta\)</span> in the framework of long-term public investments. In [64] simple, intergenerationally acceptable bounds are given for <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0182">\(\rho\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0183">\(\gamma.\)</span> In the light of the following considerations these bound are not fully convincing.</p>

<p>In the economic and ecological literature the adequacy of the Ramseyan model has been seriously questioned. So-called overlapping generation models or generation adjusted discounting models are advocated instead. The main idea is to discount for living generations at any time at the rate in eq. (11) with <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0184">\(\rho&gt;0,\)</span> i.e. with <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0185">\(\gamma=\rho+\epsilon\delta,\)</span> but diminish the rate for all yet unborn generations down to <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0186">\(\epsilon\delta,\)</span> thus also facilitating the transition into a sustainable state of economy. Accordingly, Bayer [9] assumed that each generation follows its own preferences, thus discounts with <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0187">\(\gamma\)</span> including the myopic part and distributes consumption (loss) effects evenly among its members. For yet unborn generations discounting is only done by <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0188">\(\epsilon\delta\)</span> and the requirement of intergenerational equity is fulfilled. This model, slightly modified in [67] , assuming the same preferences for all generations then produces an equivalent time-variant discount rate (discrete discounting) for a consumption effect <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0189">\(c_m\)</span> at time <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0190">\(m\)</span></p>

(lqi-display-013)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[\gamma_E'(m)=\left(\frac{c_m}{PV_{OLAG}(m)}\right)^{1/m}-1\tag{12}\]
```

<p>where the present value for this consumption effect for overlapping age groups (OLAG) can be determined from</p>

(lqi-display-014)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[PV_{OLAG}(m)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{a=0}^{m-1}\frac{c_mh(a,n)}{(1+\epsilon\delta)^{m-a}(1+\rho+\epsilon\delta)^a}+\sum_{a=m}^{a_u}\frac{c_mh(a,n)}{(1+\rho+\epsilon\delta)^m}&\text{for }m\leq a_u\\\displaystyle\sum_{a=0}^{a_u}\frac{c_mh(a,n)}{(1+\epsilon\delta)^{m-a}(1+\rho+\epsilon\delta)^a}&\text{for }m>a_u\end{cases}\tag{13}\]
```

<p><span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0191">\(a_u\)</span> is the largest age considered in the life table and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0192">\(h(a,n)\)</span> the age distribution of the population <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0193">\((n=\)</span> population growth rate). <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0194">\(\gamma_E&#x27;(m)\)</span> can be shown to converge to <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0195">\(\epsilon\delta\)</span> for <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0196">\(m\to\infty.\)</span> The rate eq. (12) can well be approximated by</p>

(lqi-display-015)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[\gamma_E'(m)\approx\epsilon\delta+\rho\exp[-am]\tag{14}\]
```

<p>with <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0197">\(a\ (\approx0.013)\)</span> a suitably chosen constant. The discount rate for continuous discounting is <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0198">\(\gamma_E(t)=\ln(1+\gamma_E&#x27;(t))\approx\gamma_E&#x27;(m).\)</span> Fig. <span id="lqi-baseline-number-7">7</span> shows that the equivalent discount rate approaches its asymptotic value after practically 200 to 300 years (solid line). The dotted line represents the discount rate without weighting by the age distribution. Clearly, the critical component still is the equivalent time preference rate function <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0199">\(\rho\exp[-am],\)</span> or more specifically, the magnitude of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0200">\(\rho,\)</span> which must be selected in accordance with economical market mechanisms. International organisations frequently determine realistic constant rates <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0201">\(\gamma\)</span> from long-term rates referring to secondary market yields on long-term bonds which range between 3 to 6% for most countries. Similar and in part stronger arguments than in [9] for a decaying interest rate have been put forward in [25] .</p>

<p>For any discounting scheme the discount rate should be the smallest possible. ## 6. Discounting Utilities and Age-averaging

<p>We are now going to imbed the foregoing derivation for the life quality index into a more general setting. Health-related economics have developed similar concepts independently and in part earlier starting with the seminal work by Usher [83] . The expected remaining present value life time utility at age <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0203">\(a\)</span> (conditional on having survived until <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0204">\(a)\)</span> for <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0205">\(\gamma(t)=\rho(t)+\delta\)</span> is (see, for example, [94] [1] [74] [75] [70] [33] [34] )</p>

(lqi-math-07)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[\begin{aligned}L(a)=E[U(a)]&=\int_a^{a_u}\frac{f(t)}{S(a)}U(a,t)\,dt\\&=\int_a^{a_u}\frac{f(t)}{S(a)}\int_a^t u[c(\tau)]\exp\left[-\left(\int_a^\tau\gamma(\theta)\,d\theta\right)\right]d\tau\,dt\\&=\frac{1}{S(a)}\int_a^{a_u}u[c(t)]\exp\left[-\left(\int_a^t\rho(\theta)\,d\theta+\delta(t-a)\right)\right]S(t)\,dt\end{aligned}\tag{15}\]
```

<p> Eq. (18) (or if eq. (3) is interpreted as an utility function) it belongs to the class of so-called iso-elastic constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) functions according to Arrow-Pratt. <p>It appears natural to use values for <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0244">\(q\)</span> consistent with the work-leisure optimization principle as outlined and verified above. If mortality is a function of some other quantity x can also represent the leading parameter in that quantity. The quantity <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0285">\(\frac{d\ell}{\ell}.\)</span> is in rough numerical agreement with an approximation proposed in [20] .</p>

<p>In health-related economics the <em>value</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0286"><em>of a</em></span> <em>statistical</em> <em>life</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0287"><span class="math notranslate nohighlight">\((VSL)\)</span><sup><a id="lqi-note-ref-7" href="#lqi-note-7" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 7">7</a></sup></span> has been defined using the <em>willingness-to-pay</em> <em>(WTP),</em> implicitely assuming that the following relationship holds:</p>

(lqi-display-027)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[WTP(a)=\frac{g}{q}\frac{d\ell_d(a,\rho,\delta)}{\ell_d(a,\rho,\delta)}\tag{25}\]
```

<p>An indifference relation similar to eq. (9) is also adopted. This has been discussed thoroughly in [34] and [12] , especially the conditions under which eq. (25) is valid. For constant consumption over the lifetime and a constant mortality reduction at all ages, i.e. <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0288">\(dm=\Delta,\)</span> eq. (25) is, in fact, a correct definition. It can be derived from the life quality index by dividing it by the marginal utility <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0289">\(u&#x27;(g)=g^{q-1}\)</span> (in order to obtain a monetary quantity). Performing age-averaging one can define the <em>societal</em> <em>value</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0290"><em>of a</em></span> <em>statistical</em> <em>life</em> <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0291">\((SVSL),\)</span> i.e.</p>

(lqi-display-028)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[SVSL=\frac{g}{q}\ell(\rho,\delta)\tag{26}\]
```

<p>and, correspondingly, the <em>societal</em> <em>willingness-to-pay</em> (SWTP)</p>

(lqi-display-029)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[SWTP=E_A\left[\frac{g}{q}\frac{d\ell_d(A,\rho,\delta)}{\ell_d(A,\rho,\delta)}\right]\tag{27}\]
```

<p>which, under otherwise the same conditions, is identical with eq. (21).</p>

<p>Both lines of thought, the economical <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0292">\((SVSL)\)</span> and the LQI approach <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0293">\((SLQI),\)</span> have a good conceptual and theoretical basis. They complement each other. In particular, the derivations for eq. (2) justify the power function form in eq. (18) and lets eq. (20) be interpreted as an expected remaining present value life time utility for all those alive at <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0294">\(t=0.\)</span> Neither criterion (21) nor (27) depend on any benefit other than risk reduction or life extension. In most applications clear support for decisions can be reached by using either of the approaches, even the one without discounting and age averaging. Age averaging is generally necessary for the technical applications we have in mind because the risk reduction intervention is to be executed at <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0295">\(t=0\)</span> for all those living now. The concept of discounting future utilities by <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0296">\((\rho+\delta)\)</span> may be debatable as the subjective time preference rate <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0297">\(\rho\)</span> is concerned but not with respect to the population and economic growth. The SLQI−based approach explicitly combines three important human concerns, that is high life expectancy, high consumption and an optimized time available for the development of one’s personality off the time for paid work. Criterion (21), having in mind its derivation, also tells us that larger expenses for risk reduction are inefficient and smaller expenses are not admissible in view of the constitutional right for life. In particular, criterion (21) is affordable from a societal point of view.</p>

## 7. Application to Technical Facilities under Event-type Hazards

### 7.1 Cost-benefit Analyses

<p>In applying criteria of the type (21) to technical facilities under event-type hazards (natural or man-made) two aspects have to be considered, the cost benefit aspect and the public safety aspect.</p>

<p>Below three representative objective functions will be given.</p>

<p>For systematic reconstruction after failure, negligibly short reconstruction times and single mode failure an appropriate objective function based on the renewal model is</p>

(lqi-display-030)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[Z(\mathbf{p})=\frac{b}{\gamma}-C(\mathbf{p})-(C(\mathbf{p})+H_M+H_F)h^*(\gamma,\mathbf{p})\tag{28}\]
```

<p>which is to be maximized. In this function <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0299">\(b\)</span> is the ( here constant) benefit per time unit, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0300">\(\gamma\)</span> the (here constant) long term market interest rate for continuous discounting, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0301">\(C(\mathbf{p})\)</span> the construction cost resp. the reconstruction cost, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0302">\(H_M\)</span> the physical damage cost, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0303">\(H_F=kN_{PE}(SHC\)</span> or <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0304">\(SLSC)\)</span> the compensation cost and</p>

(lqi-display-031)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[h^*(\gamma,\mathbf{p})=\frac{f^*(\gamma,\mathbf{p})}{1-f^*(\gamma,\mathbf{p})}\tag{29}\]
```

<p>the Laplace transform of the renewal intensity or failure rate with <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0305">\(f^*(\gamma,\mathbf{p})=\int_0^\infty\exp[-\gamma t]f(t,\mathbf{p})\,dt\)</span> the Laplace transform of the density <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0306">\(f(t,\mathbf{p})\)</span> of the times between failures. The compensation cost <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0307">\(H_F\)</span> will be discussed below.</p>

<p>If, in particular, there is a stationary stream of adverse Poissonian events (earthquakes, storms, fires, etc.) with occurrence rate <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0308">\(\lambda\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0309">\(P_f(\mathbf{p})\)</span> the failure probability in an adverse event it is (see [71] and [62] )</p>

(lqi-display-032)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[h^*(\gamma,\mathbf{p})=\frac{\lambda P_f(\mathbf{p})}{\gamma}\tag{30}\]
```

<p>so that <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0310">\(r(\mathbf{p})=\lambda P_f(\mathbf{p})\)</span> is the failure rate.</p>

<p>If the facility is given up after failure or a finite service time <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0311">\(t_s\)</span> we have [78]</p>

(lqi-display-033)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[Z(\mathbf{p})=b\int_0^{t_s}\frac{1-\exp[-\gamma t]}{\gamma}f_1(t,\mathbf{p})\,dt-C(\mathbf{p})-(H_M+H_F)\int_0^{t_s}\exp[-\gamma t]f_1(t,\mathbf{p})\,dt\tag{31}\]
```

<p>where <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0312">\(f_1(t,\mathbf{p})\)</span> is the density of the time to first failure.</p>

<p>For aging structures (increasing risk function) with preventive maintenance by perfect repairs at regular intervals <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0313">\(a\)</span> (age replacement strategy) it is [78]</p>

(lqi-display-034)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[Z_M(\mathbf{p},a)=\frac{b}{\gamma}-C(\mathbf{p})-\frac{(C(\mathbf{p})+H_M+H_F)f^{**}(\gamma,\mathbf{p},a)+R(\mathbf{p})\exp[-\gamma a]\bar{F}(\mathbf{p},a)}{1-\bigl(f^{**}(\gamma,\mathbf{p},a)+\exp[-\gamma a]\bar{F}(\mathbf{p},a)\bigr)}\tag{32}\]
```

<p>where <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0314">\(f^{**}(\gamma,\mathbf{p},a)=\int_0^a\exp[-\gamma t]f(t,\mathbf{p})\,dt\)</span> is the incomplete Laplace transform of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0315">\(f(t,\mathbf{p}),\ \bar{F}(\mathbf{p},a)\)</span> the probability of survival up to <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0316">\(a\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0317">\(R(\mathbf{p})\)</span> the repair cost. <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0318">\(R(\mathbf{p})\)</span> usually is much smaller than the failure cost <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0319">\(C(\mathbf{p})+H_M+H_F\ [79].\)</span> Here, (perfect) repairs (renewals) are undertaken either after failure or at age <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0320">\(a.\)</span> Eqs. (28), (31) and (32) are only examples of a rich spectrum of objective functions based on the renewal model (see [78] and [79] ). For example, multiple mode failures, non-constant benefit rates, block replacements, renewals following indicative inspections etc. can be handled. Non-constant discounting is discussed in [67] .</p>

<p>In accordance with economic theory benefits and (expected) cost should be discounted by the same rate. While the owner or operator may take interest rates from the financial market the assessment of the interest rate for an cost-benefit optimization in the name of the public is difficult. The requirement that any objective function must be non-negative leads immediately to the conclusion that constant interest rates must have an upper bound <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0321">\(\gamma_{\max}\)</span> depending on the benefit rate <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0322">\(b\approx\beta C(\mathbf{p})\)</span> (see [28] ). For example, a special objective derivable from eq. (28) is:</p>

(lqi-display-035)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[\frac{\beta C(\mathbf{p})}{\gamma}-C(\mathbf{p})-(C(\mathbf{p})+H_M+H_F)\frac{\lambda(\mathbf{p})}{\gamma}\geq0\tag{33}\]
```

<p>where <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0323">\(C(\mathbf{p})\)</span> are (re-)construction cost, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0324">\(H\)</span> the damage cost, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0325">\(\lambda(\mathbf{p})\)</span> the (Poissonian) failure rate and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0326">\(\mathbf{p}\)</span> some parameter. Therefore, by solving the equality for <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0327">\(\gamma\)</span> and given (optimal) <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0328">\(\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{p}^*\)</span></p>

(lqi-display-036)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[\gamma<\gamma_{\max}<\beta-\lambda(\mathbf{p})\left(1+\frac{H_M+H_F}{C(\mathbf{p})}\right)\tag{34}\]
```

<p>implying <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0329">\(\gamma&lt;\beta\)</span> for <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0330">\(\lambda(\mathbf{p})\ll\beta.\)</span> It follows that the benefit rate <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0331">\(\beta\)</span> must be slightly larger than <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0332">\(\gamma_{\max}.\)</span> The public benefit rate is closely related to the real per capita economic growth. From eq. (33) one also concludes that there must be <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0333">\(\gamma&gt;0\)</span> because the limit <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0334">\(\gamma\to0^+\)</span> is <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0335">\(\pm\infty\)</span> or at least undefined. Generalization to time-dependent benefit and/or interest rates is straightforward.</p>

<p>The quantities <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0336">\(N_{PE}\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0337">\(k\)</span> in the compensation cost are the number of potentially endangered people and the probability of being killed in a failure event, respectively. They can only be estimated reliably for a specific project. <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0338">\(k\)</span> must be estimated taking account of the cause of failure, the severity and suddenness of failure, the possibilities for escape, possibly availability and functionality of warning and rescue systems, etc. The probability <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0339">\(k\)</span> can vary between less than 1/10000 and 1. For example, estimates show <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0340">\(k=0.01\)</span> to <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0341">\(0.1\)</span> for earthquakes [17] , <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0342">\(k=10^{-6}\)</span> to <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0343">\(0.1\)</span> for floods [36] , <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0344">\(k=0.1\)</span> for building fires [24] and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0345">\(k=0.02\)</span> or less to <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0346">\(0.7\)</span> for large fires in road tunnels [52] . A summary of the estimation methodology is given in [37] . <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0347">\(N_{PE}\)</span> is not the maximum possible number of people in a unit but the expected number in an event by taking into account their time-averaged presence. For example, for living houses and flats one would expect around 0.5 persons per room, for hospitals 1 to 2 beds per room but for retail premises 10 to 20 persons per 100 m<span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0348"><sup>2</sup></span> gross sales area. Neglect of compensation cost <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0349">\(H_F\)</span> in eq. (28), (31) and (32) is ethically indefensible.</p>

<p>The legal situation for criteria of the type (21) for the owner or operator of a facility is <strong>absolute</strong> (strict) liability. The case of liability (due to negligence and/or intention) is not of interest since liability and compensations for loss of life and health is a matter for the courts. For absolute liability owners or operators additionally have to consider that they themselves or their insurance can compensate the dependents of the victims in an accident (plus, possibly, compensation for non-pecuniary damage). This is essentially the value of the lost earnings or the so-called societal human capital [64] . It is computed from</p>

(lqi-display-037)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[SHC=g\int_0^{a_u}\ell(a)h(a,n)\,da\approx g\frac{\ell}{2}\tag{35}\]
```

<p>where <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0350">\(\ell(a)=\ell_d(a,0,0)\)</span> the remaining life expectancy at age <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0351">\(a.\)</span> It is useful to average over the age-distribution <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0352">\(h(a,n).\)</span> Alternatively, one can integrate eq. (10) to obtain the societal compensation cost</p>

(lqi-display-038)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[SLSC=\int_0^{a_u}LSC(\ell(a))h(a,n)\,da\approx LSC(\tfrac\ell2)\approx SHC\tag{36}\]
```

<p>with</p>

(lqi-display-039)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[LSC(\ell_r)=g\left[1-\left(1+\frac{\ell_r}{\ell}\right)^{-\frac1q}\right]\ell_r\tag{37}\]
```

<p>and where <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0353">\(\ell_r\)</span> is the number of life years saved. It is important that the remaining life expectancy in an event is undiscounted in order to avoid double discounting. Depending on the legal and taxation conditions <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0354">\(SHC\)</span> or <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0355">\(SLSC\)</span> may also be computed with the full GDP. Small variations in <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0356">\(q\)</span> are numerically insignificant.</p>

### 7.2 Risk Acceptance Criteria

<p>Society, however, has to protect life and health of an anonymous person against all involuntary risks. For technical facilities the change in life expectancy or mortality must be replaced by a change in the failure rate, i.e. [64]</p>

(lqi-display-040)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[dm=k\,dr(\mathbf{p},t)\tag{38}\]
```

<p>where <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0357">\(k\)</span> is the probability of being killed in or by the facility in an adverse event and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0358">\(r(\mathbf{p},t)\)</span> the failure rate dependent on a parameter set <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0359">\(\mathbf{p}.\)</span></p>

<p>From renewal theory it is known that the (unconditional) failure rate corresponding to the renewal intensity is (dependance on <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0360">\(\mathbf{p}\)</span> ommitted for the moment)</p>

(lqi-display-041)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[r(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f_i(t)\tag{39}\]
```

<p>where <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0361">\(f_i(t)\)</span> is the density function of the time to the i-th renewal (failure). As pointed out in [19] <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0362">\(r(t)\)</span> has a limit <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0363">\(1/E[T]\)</span> for <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0364">\(f(t)\to0\)</span> if <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0365">\(t\to\infty\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0366">\(E[T]\)</span> the mean time between failures. In approaching the limit it can be strictly increasing, strictly decreasing or oscillate in a damped manner around <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0367">\(1/E[T].\)</span> Ordinary renewal processes then tend to be large around <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0368">\(t=E[T],2E[T],\ldots\)</span> and small around <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0369">\(t=0,\tfrac32E[T],\tfrac53E[T],\ldots.\)</span> The oscillations die out more rapidly for larger dispersions of the failure distribution. In many examples oscillations have been found when the risk function is increasing. Unfortunately, eq. (39) has closed-form solutions only for very few special failure models (for example, for the exponential distribution function [19] , the normal distribution function [26] , the Erlang distribution function [57] and the Weibull distribution function [76] ) which, at most, may be used as approximations for realistic failure models. A particularly suitable</p>

(lqi-math-10)=
<div class="native-source-content" id="lqi-math-10" data-source-math="lqi-math-10">

<p>numerical method for the calculation of the renewal function of other, possibly only numerical distribution functions <span class="math notranslate nohighlight">\(F(t)\)</span> for <span class="math notranslate nohighlight">\(H(t)=E[N(t)]=F(t)+\int_0^t H(t-u)\,dF(u)\)</span> is proposed in [5] from which <span class="math notranslate nohighlight">\(r(t)=\frac{dH(t)}{dt}\)</span> (see also [79] ).</p>

</div>

<p>However, there are two simple approximate results. For quite general failure models there is an asymptotic result</p>

(lqi-display-042)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[r(\mathbf{p})=\lim_{t\to\infty}r(\mathbf{p},t)=\frac1{E[T]}\text{ if }\lim_{t\to\infty}f(t)=0\tag{40}\]
```

<p>For aging components with increasing risk function the following bounds on the renewal function are given in [6] p. 54</p>

(lqi-display-043)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[\frac{t}{E[T]}-1\leq\frac{t}{\int_0^t(1-F(\tau))\,d\tau}-1\leq H(t)\leq\frac{tF(t)}{\int_0^t(1-F(\tau))\,d\tau}\leq\frac{t}{E[T]}\tag{41}\]
```

<p>The upper bound in eq. (41) turns out to be remarkably close to the exact result for small <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0370">\(t.\)</span> Under suitable conditions one also has</p>

(lqi-display-044)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[\begin{aligned}r(t)&=\frac{d}{dt}H(t)\leq\frac{d}{dt}\frac{tF(t)}{\int_0^t(1-F(\tau))\,d\tau}=\\&=\frac1{\int_0^t(1-F(\tau))\,d\tau}\left(F(t)+tf(t)-\frac{tF(t)(1-F(t))}{\int_0^t(1-F(\tau))\,d\tau}\right)\end{aligned}\tag{42}\]
```

<p>Again, the upper bound for eq. (42) is found to be very close to the exact result up to approximately <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0371">\(E[T].\)</span> It also approaches the limit <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0372">\(1/E[T]\)</span> for large <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0373">\(t.\)</span> The lower bound obtainable from eq. (41) by differentiation is generally less useful.</p>

<p>For systematically renewed but not maintained facilities it is now proposed to use the asymptotic failure rate <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0374">\(r(\mathbf{p},t)=r(\mathbf{p},t\to\infty)\)</span> in eq. (40) and eq. (38) motivated by the constancy of the failure rate for large <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0375">\(t.\)</span> For Poissonian failure processes it is exactly constant for all times. For most other failure processes of practical interest the failure rate rapidly approaches its constant asymptotic value starting from zero. For facilities given up after failure or after a finite service time one needs to compute <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0376">\(r(\mathbf{p},t_s).\)</span> Finally, for facilities with preventive maintenance at regular intervals as in eq. (32) one should compute <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0377">\(r(\mathbf{p},a),\)</span> either numerically or by the upper bound eq. (42). It may be debated whether one even should use some time average in the last two cases.</p>

<p>Making use of eq. (21) this results for a given project, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0378">\(N_{PE}\)</span> persons potentially endangered and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0379">\(k\)</span> the probability of being killed in the failure event in criteria of the type</p>

(lqi-display-045)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[\begin{aligned}dC_Y(\mathbf{p})&=-dg\geq-\frac{g}{q}E_A\left[\frac{d\ell_d(A,\rho,\delta)}{\ell_d(A,\rho,\delta)}\right]N_{PE}\\&\approx-\frac{g}{q}C_{\Delta\bar{\ell}}N_{PE}dm=-G_{\Delta\bar{\ell}}N_{PE}dm=-G_{\Delta\bar{\ell}}kN_{PE}dr(\mathbf{p},t)\end{aligned}\tag{43}\]
```

<p>where <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0380">\(dC_Y(\mathbf{p})\)</span> are the incremental yearly cost for a risk reduction by <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0381">\(dr(\mathbf{p}).\)</span> This quantity may also be denoted by <em>societal</em> <em>willingness-to-pay</em> per year <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0382">\((SWTP).\)</span> In particular, the quantity <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0383">\(G_{\Delta\bar{\ell}}\)</span> may also be interpreted as the <em>societal</em> <em>value</em> <span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0384"><em>of a</em></span> <em>statistical</em> <em>life</em> <em>(</em> <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0385">\(SVSL)\)</span> (see eq. (23)). Age-averaged willingness-to-pay is the correct quantity to use as it must be assumed that a representative crosssection of the population is endangered by the event-type hazard <sup><a id="lqi-note-ref-8" href="#lqi-note-8" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 8">8</a></sup>. Eq. (43) has to be applied for each hazard and failure mode if there are more than one <sup><a id="lqi-note-ref-9" href="#lqi-note-9" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 9">9</a></sup>.</p>

<p>It is important to note that the additional risk reducing investment <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0403">\(dC_Y(\mathbf{p})\)</span> depends on the change <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0404">\(dr(\mathbf{p})\)</span> of the failure rate and not on its absolute value. If <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0405">\(C_Y(p)\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0406">\(r(p)\)</span> are scalar and differentiable one also has <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0407">\(dC_Y(p)=\frac{dC_Y(p)}{dp}\,dp\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0408 lqi-inline-0409">\(dr(p,t)=\frac{dr(p,t)}{dp} dp.\)</span> Division of both sides of eq. (43) by <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0410">\(dp\)</span> tells us how it can be solved in applications, i.e. by <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0411 lqi-inline-0412">\(\frac{dC_Y(p)}{dp}\geq-G_{\Delta\bar{\ell}}kN_{PE}\frac{dr(p,t)}{dp} .\)</span> For a vectorial parameter <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0413">\(\mathbf{p}\)</span> one best solves the criterion as an optimization task</p>

(lqi-display-048)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[\textbf{Minimize: }S(\mathbf{p},t)=C(\mathbf{p})+G_xkN_{PE}r(\mathbf{p},t)\tag{44}\]
```

<p>recognizing that eq. (43) is the first-order optimality condition of eq. (44) if no cost-benefit optimization is performed. Cost-benefit optimal facilities may or may not fulfill criterion (43)<sup><a id="lqi-note-ref-10" href="#lqi-note-10" role="doc-noteref" aria-label="Footnote 10">10</a></sup>.</p>

## 8. Risk Reduction for Toxic Substances

<p>For continuously effective, ubiquitous, environmental pollutants in air, soil or water <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0414">\(dm\)</span> or <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0415">\(\frac{d\ell_d(A,\rho,\delta)}{\ell_d(A,\rho,\delta)}\)</span> usually are related to the dose of those exposed and risk reduction measures work on limiting or reducing the dose [38] . Frequently, the dose accumulates over time increasing mortality accordingly. Also, latency has to be considered appropriately and there may be a delay time until an (monetary) intervention shows effect [34] . Additional considerations about the emiting source, the transport mechanisms and the exposure to the hazard have to be performed. Let, as a simple example,</p>

(lqi-display-049)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[dm(a)=f(x)=\kappa x\begin{cases}a-T&\mathit{for}\ a>T\\0&\mathit{for}\ a\leq T\end{cases}\tag{45}\]
```

<p>be the dose-mortality relationship with <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0416">\(x\)</span> the dose and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0417">\(k\)</span> the so-called slope factor, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0418">\((a-T)\)</span> takes account of the cumulative nature of exposition to a time-invariant dosis and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0419">\(T\)</span> is a deterministic latency period. A deterministic latency period clearly is an approximation but data are almost completely missing for a better model. Furthermore, <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0420">\(D\)</span> is the (deterministic) delay time between payments for risk reduction and the time where they become effective. Eq. (21) is a convenient starting point. In fact, the only difference is a slightly changed relation of the type eq. (24). For example, eq. (24) changes for a cumulative dose according to eq. (45) and additive mortality changes, i.e. <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0421">\(\mu_\Sigma(a)=\mu(a)+dm(a),\)</span> into</p>

(lqi-display-050)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[C_{\Delta\bar{\ell}}(\rho,\delta,D,T)=\int_0^{a_u-D}\frac{\frac{\ell_d(\rho,\delta,a,T,D,y)-\ell_d(\rho,\delta,a,T,D,0)}{y}}{\ell_d(\rho,\delta,a)}h(a,n)\,da\tag{46}\]
```

<p>with</p>

(lqi-display-051)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[\ell_d(\rho,\delta,a,T,D,y)=\int_{a+D}^{a_u}\exp\left[-\left(\int_a^t\rho(\theta)\,d\theta+\delta(t-a)+\int_{a+D}^t\kappa y\begin{cases}\theta-T&\mathit{for}\ \theta>T\\0&\mathit{for}\ \theta\leq T\end{cases}d\theta\right)\right]dt\]
```

<p>if it is assumed that payments for risk reduction start at the decision point. If payments start after the delay time the integral over the discount rate in eq. (46) starts at <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0422">\(a+D.\)</span> But there is a large spectrum of conditions under which exposition to toxic substances occurs, accumulates and causes fatalities. And there are different kinds of mortality reduction schemes in use including proportional mortality reductions.</p>

## 9. Predictive Cohort Life Tables

<p>Setting risk acceptability criteria also has a sustainability aspect because the next or even the far future is concerned. Period life tables have been used in [63] and [66] . So-called cohort life tables certainly would be better as they reflect the common trend towards larger life expectancies and more compact age distributions. Since we are interested in future risks we have to extrapolate into the future. Time- and age-dependent mortalities can be obtained by extrapolating from a sequence</p>

(lqi-figures-09)=
<div class="source-display" id="lqi-figures-09" style="--source-width:23.307em">

[![Figure, original typesetting, source PDF page 26](images/lqi-figures-09.png)](images/lqi-figures-09.png)

<p>of historical period life tables so that</p>

(lqi-display-054)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[\mu_{\vartheta,y}(a)=\mu_y(a)b(a)^{\vartheta+a-y}\tag{48}\]
```

<p>where <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0442">\(y\)</span> is the reference year, i.e. the last year for which a period table is available and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0443">\(\vartheta\lessgtr y\)</span> is the year of birth. Unfortunately, cohort life tables do exist only for a few countries. Cohort life tables, for example, yield about 7% larger life expectancies at present. Predictive cohort table must be constructed, for example for <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0444">\(y=\vartheta=2000\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0445">\(0\leq a\leq110.\)</span> Results are collected in table 7 for thirteen developed countries. An uninterrupted sequence of period life tables must be available for at least the last 50 years but not longer than 100 years so that extrapolations for the age dependent mortalities can be performed. Although even longer sequences of period tables are available, only tables for the last 100 years are used in order to take account of the reduced infant mortality. The data used are all from [13] where some more countries are included. Clearly, such extrapolations are based on the assumption that the observed demographic trends continue throughout the next 100 years. Trends in all other demographic parameters are not taken into account but must be expected. Their effect, however, is small. For example, if population growth <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0446">\(n\)</span> is set to zero resulting in a small change in the age structure of a population no noticeable change in <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0447">\(G_{\Delta\bar{\ell}}\)</span> is observed.</p>

## 12. Empirical Verification of Constant in Acceptability Criterion

<p>There have been many attempts to estimate this quantity indirectly (among the rich literature for this subject see, for example, [86] , [87] , [88] , [80] , [48] and [43] ), mostly by estimating the cost of some life saving operation like limiting highway speed [3] , installing smoke detectors in homes, using seat belts in cars or purchasing bicycle helmets. Also, the compensation in risky jobs by higher wages has been used [88] as well as surveys with respect to hypothetical risky situations, so-called contingent valuation studies [59] [32] . Also, revealed preference methods have been used. The values reported are between less than 1 Mill. US$ and more than 10 Mill. US$, i.e. more than 2 to 20 times as much as the (undiscounted) value of average lost earnings in case of a fatal accident at mid life. Tengs et al. [80] find cost of less than 1000 US$ up to 100 Mill. US$ for a large number of life saving interventions. Health related investments cluster around 4 to 6 Mill. US$ [87] . Studies in the US tend to give somewhat larger values than European studies but not without exceptions. Contingent valuation studies tend to give about the same or slightly larger mean values but larger spread. Market studies (buying bicycle helmets, cigarette smoking) gave the smallest value. When comparing numbers one has to keep in mind that they have to be adjusted to year 2000. For example, values for year 1995 should be multiplied by 1.1 and values for year 1985 by 1.6. The studies [89] , [49] , [48] and [11] among several other similar studies are so-called meta-analyses, i.e. analyses across several other studies. They should be especially valuable for our purpose. The studies in [89] , [49] , and [11] contain 60, 33 and 25, respectively, other studies. In fig. 12 and 13 some results collected in [89] and [49] are presented graphically showing means betwenn 6 and 8 Mill. US$ and the large scatter of the estimates. The distributions of VSL are highly skewed so that the corresponding medians are some 30% smaller. The estimates in [49] based on wage differentials in the USA and recomputed in [89] are significantly smaller in contrast to the findings in [90] . Their own estimates by Mrozek/Taylor [49] found a range between 1.5 and 2.5 Mill. US$. The correlation coefficient as a crude measure of the dependence of VSL (or VOSL) on income (or GDP) is also given in the figures.</p>

<p>Higher background mortality (smokers or other) decreases the VSL as well as higher risk levels which is not in accordance with the theory. Latency can decrease the WTP’s considerably. WT- P’s for private goods (for example, safe cars) appear to be larger as compared with public goods. Viscusi/Aldy [89] discuss in depth why estimates depend on quite a number of factors and are different for compensating wage differentials, public health interventions and product markets. De Baeij et al. [11] also find large differences even in the narrow field of road safety (see Fig. 14). The variability in [89] or [11] may in large part be explained by differences in the methodologies used to estimate the value of life and which still are intensely debated in the relevant literature. But apart from methodological differences one has to recognize that individual and public risk perception, the leading factor in many studies, and the corresponding WTP’s for the reduction of risks in the different areas must simply be considered as highly subjective. In addition, and this appears very obvious, many estimates are based on in part strongly biased samples (higher job risks are generally taken by healthy, male and young adults).</p>

<p>Some uncertainty exists about the precise meaning of the reported values of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0508">\(VSL.\)</span> It appears as if the definition of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0509">\(VSL\)</span> is not everywhere the same but the majority of studies starts from the definition in eq. (25) with <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0510">\(a=0,\)</span> mostly in the form <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0511">\(VSL=WTP/dm.\)</span> Furthermore, it is rarely clear whether the empirical estimates take account of the age-dependency, consider age-averaging and/or a particular mortality reduction scheme and the level of risk reduction. It is also unclear whether discounting is at all considered and, if, at which rate. Nevertheless, if we simply take <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0512">\(VSL=\frac{g}{q}\ell,\)</span> the definition in eq. (26) without age-averaging and discounting for <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0513">\(a=0,\)</span> there is excellent agreement in the means between theoretical and empirical estimates. But this observation may be just coincidental. The non-compatibility with some empirical estimates does not make our theory obsolete in view of the above remarks about biases in the data, methodological differences and the question of discounting.</p>

<p>Even if one considers the variations in <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0514">\(g\)</span> and <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0515">\(w\)</span> for the different countries it is clear that table 7 gives much smaller spread of the constants <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0516">\(G_\Delta=\frac{g}{q}C_\Delta\)</span> or <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0517">\(G_{\Delta\bar{\ell}}\)</span> or similar than the empirical estimates. Comparing our theoretical results with the empirical estimates shows reasonable agreement in the mean of the value of <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0518">\(G_\Delta\)</span>,especially if computed with the full GDP, although <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0519">\(G_\Delta\)</span> still falls into the lower half of the empirical estimates. Remember the constant <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0520">\(C_\Delta\)</span> is about 2.5-times larger than <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0521">\(C_{\Delta\bar{\ell}}.\)</span> Age-averaging and discounting are responsible for the lower <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0522">\(G_{\Delta\bar{\ell}}\)</span> or similar.</p>

## 13. Conclusions

<p>This study attempts to summarize recent findings how to set risk acceptability criteria in terms of life expectancy and economic parameters. Moreover, it provides a theoretical foundation for rationalizing the apparent huge diversity in empirical estimates of values of life savings, based on the willingness to pay approach. Many partial aspects of the theory are verified by empirical evidence so that the theory can be considered as sufficiently complete. So far the empirical findings in the last section neither support fully our theory nor can it be dismissed on the same grounds. What can be said is that the theory is unaffected by subjective factors, is rational, affordable and in agreement with ethical standards. Extreme catastrophic events are excluded if they are capable of changing significantly the economy or the structure of the population in a region. Such events are thinkable, examples being meteorite impact, earthquakes with magnitude beyond 8.5 in highly populated areas or the maximum credible accident in a chemical or nuclear plant. They require additional considerations.</p>

<ul id="lqi-conclusions-list">

<li><p><span hidden aria-hidden="true" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0526">•</span>There are two independently developed approaches for the quantification of the (societal) willingness-to-pay for risk reduction that is the health-related economical approach as advocated and outlined, for example, by Shepard/Zeckhauser [75] and the approach based on the societal life quality index <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0527">\((SLQI)\)</span> as advocated by Nathwani et al. [44] and Pandey/Nathwani [55] . Although slightly different in the derivations they complement each other and lead to the essentially same numerical results under otherwise identical parameter assumptions and mortality reduction schemes.</p></li>

<li><p><span hidden aria-hidden="true" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0528">•</span>The key quantity is the societal willingnes-to-pay for risk reduction. It is given as </p>

(lqi-display-055)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[SWTP=\frac{g}{q}E_A\left[\frac{d\ell_d(A,\rho,\delta)}{\ell_d(A,\rho,\delta)}\right]\approx\frac{g}{q}C_{x\bar{\ell}}dm\]
```

<p> It is proportional to the monetary amount <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0529">\(g\)</span> available for risk reduction and the size of the risk reduction <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0530">\(dm\)</span> (as a mortality reduction) and inversely proportional to a risk aversion parameter <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0531">\(q\)</span> expressing an optimized ratio of labor and leisure time. The proportionality constant <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0532">\(C_{x\bar{\ell}}\)</span> depends on economic and demographic factors but especially on the specific mortality reduction scheme <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0533">\(x\)</span> of a life saving intervention. The quantity <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0534">\(\frac{g}{q}C_{x\bar{\ell}}\)</span> may be interpreted as societal value of a statistical life.</p></li>

<li><p><span hidden aria-hidden="true" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0535">•</span>One important parameter to be used is the part of the GDP available for public risk reduction. The GDP is considered as the presently best measure of welfare and wealth in an economical sense. Clearly, the part used for investments in order to renew deteriorating production means is not available if intra- and intergenerational equity is required. Whether the part of the GDP usually used by the public (state and communities) for internal and external safety, education, protection of national heritage and similar public duties can also be used can be debated. However, this may be interpreted as a component of life quality not directly covered by either of the approaches. Then, it must also be deducted from the GDP. The minimum available for risk reduction is the part of the GDP for private use. It is also debatable whether the amount available for risk reduction is only the excess of some minimum subsistence level of consumption because a level below is the same as being dead.</p></li>

<li><p><span hidden aria-hidden="true" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0536">•</span>Another important parameter is survival probability or life expectancy. If only event-type hazards are considered at the decision point at least all living at the decision point should be considered. This implies that predictive cohort life tables should be used. The time-horizon to be considered determines the prediction time. As a first approximation a time of around 100 years is proposed as prediction time. If event-type hazards to technical objects are considered remaining life expectancies should be considered and averaging needs to be performed over the affected age-groups in order to fulfill the intragenerational equity requirement. Toxic risks might require slightly different treatment.</p></li>

<li><p><span hidden aria-hidden="true" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0537">•</span>The third critical parameter is the effective life working time of the generations living up to the time-horizon. It is proposed to start from the present yearly working hours in official sources, for example in OECD sources. Travel times per working day between home and work may be added or not. In applications the fact that only a part, although the larger one, i.e about 70%, of the national production is due to labor should be taken into account.</p></li>

<li><p><span hidden aria-hidden="true" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0538">•</span>Discounting of future consumption is necessary for a growing (decreasing) population and a growing (decreasing ) economy as well as for the other reasons of discounting. Exponential discounting should be performed by real time-averaged rates. This discounting should also fulfill sustainability requirements (intra- and intergenerational equity). Thereby, it may be assumed that future generations have the same preferences as the present generation. The minimum (real) discount rate is equal to the time-averaged economic growth rate per capita.</p></li>

<li><p><span hidden aria-hidden="true" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0539">•</span>The specific mortality reduction scheme for man-made or natural hazards is an important parameter. However, only schemes with constant mortality reductions at all ages can be considered as intragenerationally equitable for man-made and natural hazards.</p></li>

<li><p><span hidden aria-hidden="true" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0540">•</span>There is no explicit discounting in acceptability criteria involving yearly payments for risk reduction.</p></li>

<li><p><span hidden aria-hidden="true" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0541">•</span>If the <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0542">\(LQI\)</span> is used as an alternative for the HDI (ranking of countries) the form eq. (8) can be recommended with <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0543">\(g=GDP\)</span> (all parameters at their present values).</p></li>

</ul>

```{math}
\[\frac{dZ(p)}{dp}=-\frac{dC(p)}{dp}\left(1+\frac{\lambda P_f(p)}{\gamma}\right)-(C(p)+H_M+H_F)\frac{\lambda}{\gamma}\frac{dP_f(p)}{dp}\]
```

<p>For <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0424">\(\lambda P_f(p)\ll\gamma\ll1\)</span> there is <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0425">\(p_{opt}&gt;p_{\lim}\)</span> or <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0426">\(\lambda P_f(p_{opt})&lt;\lambda P_f(p_{\lim})\)</span> if <span class="math notranslate nohighlight" data-source-math="lqi-inline-0427">\(\frac{C(p)+H_M+H_F}{\gamma}\geq G_{\Delta\bar{\ell}}kN_{PE}.\)</span> This is frequently the case.</p>

</aside>

<aside id="lqi-note-11" role="doc-footnote" aria-label="Footnote 11">

<p><a href="#lqi-note-ref-11" role="doc-backlink" aria-label="Return to footnote 11 reference"><span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0459"><sup>11</sup></span></a> It is interesting to use the concept eq. (49) to compute the life working time in years from</p>

(lqi-math-04)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[\frac{\text{labor force}}{\text{population}}\text{ ywh per employee}=\frac{\text{life working time}}{\text{life expectancy}}\text{ ywh per employee}\]
```

<p>For example, for the USA one calculates 39 years, for Germany 38.6 years, for Japan 43.2 years but for the Netherlands only 30.8 years based on data in [51] . However, the data for the Netherlands are for dependent employment only and do not include overtime work.</p>

</aside>

<aside id="lqi-note-12" role="doc-footnote" aria-label="Footnote 12">

<p><a href="#lqi-note-ref-12" role="doc-backlink" aria-label="Return to footnote 12 reference"><span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0486"><sup>12</sup></span></a> In particular, the work time estimate for the Netherlands in [51] appears unrealistically low. Also, the data for the USA are probably too high as compared to the other countries.</p>

</aside>

<aside id="lqi-note-13" role="doc-footnote" aria-label="Footnote 13">

<p><a href="#lqi-note-ref-13" role="doc-backlink" aria-label="Return to footnote 13 reference"><span data-source-math="lqi-inline-0525"><sup>13</sup></span></a> Income elasticity of a quantity is defined as the relative change in that quantity divided by the relative change in income. Therefore,</p>

(lqi-math-02)=
```{math}
:nowrap:

\[\text{income elasticity}=\frac{\frac{dSVSL}{SVSL}}{\frac{dg}{g}}=\frac{dSVSL}{dg}\frac{g}{SVSL}=1\]
```

</aside>
